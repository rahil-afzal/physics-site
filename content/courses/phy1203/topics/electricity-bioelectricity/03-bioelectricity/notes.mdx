## 1. Ohmic Current and the Nernst Potential

### 1.1 Ohmic (Driving-Force) Current Through Ion Channels

The current through an ion channel is not simply $V/R$. What matters is not the membrane potential alone, but how far the membrane potential is from the equilibrium potential of that particular ion.

<Callout type="note" title="Result: Ohmic Current Through an Ion Channel">

$$
I_x = g_x\,(V_m - E_x)
$$

- $I_x$ — current of ion $x$ (A); positive = outward current
- $g_x$ — membrane conductance for ion $x$ ($\mathrm{S} = \mathrm{A/V}$ = siemens)
- $V_m$ — membrane potential (V)
- $E_x$ — Nernst (equilibrium) potential for ion $x$ (V)

</Callout>

<Callout type="note" title="Analogy: Driving Force as Net Pressure">

Think of ion current like water through a pipe where both a pump (concentration gradient) and gravity (electrical gradient) are acting. If the pump exactly cancels gravity, there is no net flow — this is the equilibrium potential $E_x$. The quantity $(V_m - E_x)$ is the **driving force**: positive driving force pushes current out; negative pushes it in. Opening more channels (increasing $g_x$) amplifies whichever direction the driving force dictates.

</Callout>

<Callout type="note" title="Note: Sign Convention and Current Direction">

- If $V_m > E_x$: driving force is positive, ion $x$ current is outward (leaving the cell, conventionally positive for a positive ion).
- If $V_m < E_x$: driving force is negative, ion $x$ current is inward.
- If $V_m = E_x$: driving force is zero; no net current even if many channels are open.

This is why $\mathrm{Na^+}$ channels, once open during an action potential, drive the membrane toward $E_{\mathrm{Na}} \approx +60\ \mathrm{mV}$ and then current stops — it cannot overshoot the Nernst potential.

</Callout>

### 1.2 The Nernst Potential

<Callout type="note" title="Derivation: Why the Nernst Potential Exists">

An ion $X$ is at equilibrium when two opposing tendencies exactly cancel:

1. **Concentration gradient:** ion $X$ diffuses from high $[X]$ to low $[X]$ (random thermal motion).
2. **Electrical gradient:** the membrane voltage creates an electric force opposing diffusion (since the diffusing ion carries charge).

At equilibrium the electrochemical potential is equal on both sides. Setting the entropy-driven diffusion energy equal to the electrical work gives the Nernst equation.

</Callout>

<Callout type="note" title="Result: Nernst Equation">

$$
E_x = \frac{RT}{zF}\ln\frac{[X]_{\text{out}}}{[X]_{\text{in}}}
$$

- $R = 8.314\ \mathrm{J/(mol\,K)}$ — gas constant
- $T$ — absolute temperature (K)
- $z$ — ionic valence (e.g. $+1$ for $\mathrm{K^+}$, $-1$ for $\mathrm{Cl^-}$, $+2$ for $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$)
- $F = 96{,}485\ \mathrm{C/mol}$ — Faraday constant

At body temperature ($T = 310\ \mathrm{K}$), $RT/F \approx 26.7\ \mathrm{mV}$, so:

$$
E_x \approx \frac{26.7}{z}\ln\frac{[X]_{\text{out}}}{[X]_{\text{in}}}\ \mathrm{mV}
\quad\text{or}\quad
\frac{61.5}{z}\log_{10}\frac{[X]_{\text{out}}}{[X]_{\text{in}}}\ \mathrm{mV}
$$

</Callout>

{/* EDIT (cross-reference): source said "from the table in Section 1"; reworded because that table is now in lecture 1 (Electrostatics, section 1.1). */}

<Callout type="example" title="Example: Nernst Potentials for Key Ions at 310 K">

Using intracellular and extracellular concentrations from the table in the Electrostatics lecture (Section 1.1):

**Potassium** $\mathrm{K^+}$ ($z=+1$):

$$
E_{\mathrm{K}} = 26.7\ln\frac{5}{140} = 26.7\times(-3.332) \approx -89\ \mathrm{mV}
$$

**Sodium** $\mathrm{Na^+}$ ($z=+1$):

$$
E_{\mathrm{Na}} = 26.7\ln\frac{145}{12} = 26.7\times2.493 \approx +67\ \mathrm{mV}
$$

**Chloride** $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ ($z=-1$):

$$
E_{\mathrm{Cl}} = \frac{26.7}{-1}\ln\frac{116}{4} = -26.7\times3.367 \approx -90\ \mathrm{mV}
$$

**Calcium** $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$ ($z=+2$):

$$
E_{\mathrm{Ca}} = \frac{26.7}{2}\ln\frac{2}{0.0001} = 13.35\times9.903 \approx +132\ \mathrm{mV}
$$

The resting membrane potential ($-70\ \mathrm{mV}$) lies between $E_{\mathrm{K}}$ and $E_{\mathrm{Na}}$, pulled toward $E_{\mathrm{K}}$ by the high resting $\mathrm{K^+}$ permeability.

</Callout>

<Figure caption="Nernst potentials of K⁺, Cl⁻, Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ compared with the resting membrane potential (bar chart from the source notes)." />

<Callout type="note" title="Biological Connection: Goldman Equation — Extending the Nernst Equation">

The resting membrane potential is not set by a single ion but by all permeable ions simultaneously. The Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz (GHK) equation generalises the Nernst equation:

$$
V_m = \frac{RT}{F}\ln\frac{P_{\mathrm{K}}[\mathrm{K^+}]_o + P_{\mathrm{Na}}[\mathrm{Na^+}]_o + P_{\mathrm{Cl}}[\mathrm{Cl^-}]_i}{P_{\mathrm{K}}[\mathrm{K^+}]_i + P_{\mathrm{Na}}[\mathrm{Na^+}]_i + P_{\mathrm{Cl}}[\mathrm{Cl^-}]_o}
$$

where $P_x$ is the permeability of ion $x$. At rest, $P_{\mathrm{K}} \gg P_{\mathrm{Na}}$, so $V_m \approx E_{\mathrm{K}}$. During an action potential, $\mathrm{Na^+}$ channels open, $P_{\mathrm{Na}}$ surges, and $V_m$ shifts rapidly toward $E_{\mathrm{Na}} \approx +67\ \mathrm{mV}$. This is the physical basis of the depolarisation phase.

</Callout>

## 2. Action Potential and Ion Dynamics

The action potential (AP) is the neuron's binary signal — an all-or-nothing electrical event that propagates without decrement along the axon. It is the physical implementation of neural computation.

<Callout type="note" title="Note: All-or-Nothing Principle">

If a stimulus depolarises the membrane beyond the threshold ($\approx -55\ \mathrm{mV}$), a full action potential fires. A smaller stimulus produces no AP at all. The AP amplitude is always the same ($\approx 110\ \mathrm{mV}$ swing). Information is encoded not in AP amplitude but in firing rate and temporal pattern.

</Callout>

### 2.1 Five Phases of the Action Potential

**Phase 1. Resting state** ($V_m \approx -70\ \mathrm{mV}$). $\mathrm{Na^+}$ and $\mathrm{K^+}$ voltage-gated channels are closed. Only leak $\mathrm{K^+}$ channels are open, maintaining resting potential near $E_{\mathrm{K}}$.

**Phase 2. Depolarisation.** A stimulus raises $V_m$ above threshold ($-55\ \mathrm{mV}$). Voltage-gated $\mathrm{Na^+}$ channels open (positive feedback: more depolarisation $\Rightarrow$ more channels open $\Rightarrow$ more depolarisation — the *Hodgkin cycle*). $V_m$ swings rapidly toward $E_{\mathrm{Na}} \approx +67\ \mathrm{mV}$, reaching $\approx +40\ \mathrm{mV}$.

**Phase 3. Repolarisation.** $\mathrm{Na^+}$ channels inactivate (a separate gate closes from the cytoplasmic side). Voltage-gated $\mathrm{K^+}$ channels open (they respond to depolarisation but with a time delay). $\mathrm{K^+}$ rushes out, pulling $V_m$ back toward $E_{\mathrm{K}} \approx -89\ \mathrm{mV}$.

**Phase 4. Hyperpolarisation (after-potential).** $\mathrm{K^+}$ channels close slowly. The excess $\mathrm{K^+}$ efflux temporarily drives $V_m$ below resting potential to $\approx -80\ \mathrm{mV}$.

**Phase 5. Return to rest.** $\mathrm{K^+}$ channels finally close; the $\mathrm{Na^+/K^+}$-ATPase pump restores ion gradients over milliseconds to seconds.

<Figure caption="Action potential waveform, membrane potential (mV) against time (ms), with threshold at −55 mV, rest at −70 mV, and the five phases labelled (figure from the source notes)." />

<Callout type="note" title="Biological Connection: Refractory Period and Signal Directionality">

During and shortly after an AP, the membrane cannot fire again. The **absolute refractory period** ($\sim1\ \mathrm{ms}$) corresponds to $\mathrm{Na^+}$ channel inactivation — the channels are physically blocked and cannot reopen regardless of stimulus strength. The **relative refractory period** ($\sim2$–$4\ \mathrm{ms}$) corresponds to $\mathrm{K^+}$ channels still open and $V_m$ below resting — a stronger stimulus is needed to reach threshold.

The refractory period is biologically crucial: it limits maximum firing rate ($\sim500$–$1000\ \mathrm{Hz}$) and ensures APs propagate only forward along the axon — the region just behind the AP front is refractory and cannot re-fire.

</Callout>

<Callout type="note" title="Biological Connection: Myelination and Saltatory Conduction">

Unmyelinated axons conduct APs at 0.5–2 m/s. Myelin (produced by Schwann cells in the PNS and oligodendrocytes in the CNS) wraps around the axon in concentric layers, dramatically increasing effective membrane resistance and decreasing capacitance — both of which speed conduction. The AP jumps between exposed nodes of Ranvier at 70–120 m/s — a 50–100$\times$ speedup.

In multiple sclerosis, demyelination slows or blocks conduction, producing the characteristic symptoms of muscle weakness, visual disturbance, and fatigue.

</Callout>

## 3. The Hodgkin–Huxley Equivalent Circuit

Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley (Nobel Prize 1963) described the neuron membrane as an electrical circuit. This model remains the gold standard of computational neuroscience.

<Figure caption="Hodgkin–Huxley equivalent circuit between outside and inside: membrane capacitance C_m in parallel with the branches g_Na (with E_Na), g_K (with E_K) and g_L (with E_L). g_Na and g_K are time-varying; g_L is constant. V_m is the voltage across the circuit (figure from the source notes)." />

<Callout type="note" title="Note: Reading the HH Circuit">

- $C_m$: membrane capacitance (lipid bilayer).
- $g_{\mathrm{Na}}(t)$: time-varying $\mathrm{Na^+}$ conductance (voltage-gated channels).
- $g_{\mathrm{K}}(t)$: time-varying $\mathrm{K^+}$ conductance.
- $E_{\mathrm{Na}}$, $E_{\mathrm{K}}$: batteries representing Nernst potentials — the source of energy driving ion currents.
- $V_m$: voltage across the capacitor (membrane potential).

The circuit equation is:

$$
C_m\frac{dV_m}{dt} = -g_{\mathrm{Na}}(V_m - E_{\mathrm{Na}}) - g_{\mathrm{K}}(V_m - E_{\mathrm{K}}) - g_L(V_m - E_L) + I_{\text{ext}}
$$

where $g_L$ is the leak conductance and $I_{\text{ext}}$ is any applied current. This single equation generates the complete action potential waveform when $g_{\mathrm{Na}}$ and $g_{\mathrm{K}}$ are described by Hodgkin–Huxley kinetics.

</Callout>
